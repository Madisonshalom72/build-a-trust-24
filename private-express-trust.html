The Private Express Trust, Explained

A private express trust is the kind of trust a person creates on purpose — deliberately, in writing, and off the public record. Here is what it is, how it differs from the trusts a court creates, and what it honestly can and cannot do.

November 15, 2026 · Build A Trust 24 — Trust Fundamentals

What a private express trust is

A private express trust is a trust that a property owner — the grantor — creates deliberately, by expressing the intent to create it in a written instrument. "Express" means the trust exists because the grantor said so, clearly and on purpose. "Private" means two things at once: the trust is made for identified private beneficiaries rather than for a public charitable purpose, and its founding document is a private contract that is not filed with a court or recorded in a public registry.

The structure itself is simple. The grantor transfers property to a trustee, who holds legal title and manages it under the written terms, for the benefit of the beneficiaries, who hold the equitable interest. One document — the trust instrument — states who fills each role, what property the trust holds, and what the trustee may and must do. An express trust is a creature of the grantor's stated intent.

Express trusts vs. the trusts a court creates

The word "express" matters because the law also recognizes trusts nobody sat down and wrote. Keeping the four categories straight prevents most of the confusion in this field:

  • Express trust. Created intentionally by the grantor's written (or, in narrow cases, declared) words. A private express trust and a charitable trust are both express trusts — the difference is who benefits.
  • Implied trust. A court's inference from the parties' conduct that a trust relationship was intended, even though nobody used the word "trust." It is implied from behavior, not written down by choice.
  • Resulting trust. Imposed by law when a transfer suggests the recipient was not meant to keep the benefit — for example, when one person pays for property but title is placed in another's name. The law "results" the benefit back toward the person who paid.
  • Constructive trust. Not a planned trust at all, but a remedy a court imposes to prevent unjust enrichment — when someone holds property obtained by fraud or breach of duty, the court treats them as a trustee for the rightful owner.

Only the first kind can be planned, drafted, and signed at your desk. The other three exist because something already went to court.

Declaration of trust vs. trust agreement

An express trust is created one of two ways, and the document's name follows the structure. When the grantor will also serve as the first trustee, the instrument is a declaration of trust: the grantor declares that they hold identified property in trust, on stated terms. When the grantor hands the property to a different person as trustee, the instrument is a trust agreement — the form Build A Trust 24 calls the Trust Indenture Agreement — signed by both the grantor and the accepting trustee. Either way, four elements must be certain or the trust fails: a competent grantor with genuine intent, identified trust property, an ascertainable beneficiary, and a trustee with real duties to perform.

What makes it "private"

A will becomes a public record the moment it enters probate. A private express trust never does. The indenture is signed and kept with the trust's own records; it is not filed with the state or recorded at the courthouse. When a bank or title company needs proof the trust exists and the trustee has authority to act, the trustee shows a short certification or memorandum of trust — a summary confirming the trust's existence, date, and the trustee's powers, without revealing beneficiaries or distribution terms. The family's names, property, and instructions stay in the trust's private ledger, where the ownership records behind each certificate of beneficial interest are also kept.

The step that makes it real: funding

A signed indenture with nothing in it is an empty container. The trust only controls property actually transferred into it — deeds re-recorded in the trustee's name for real estate, accounts retitled, assignments signed for business interests and personal property, each item listed on the schedule of assets. An unfunded private express trust is one of the most common failures in estate planning: perfect paperwork, and a probate estate anyway, because the assets never moved. Funding is not a technicality; it is the act that gives the document something to govern.

The honest limits

A private express trust is a legitimate ownership and succession tool. It is not a cloak. It does not erase taxes — a revocable trust's income is generally taxed to the grantor, and irrevocable trusts file their own returns. It does not cancel judgments, debts, or child support, and a trust you create and control offers little shelter from your own creditors in most states. Transfers made to delay or defraud existing creditors can be unwound as fraudulent transfers, which is why a trust is a before-trouble move, set up in calm weather for organization, continuity, and privacy — never as an escape hatch after a claim already exists.

Frequently asked questions

Is a private express trust legal in every state?

Express trusts are recognized in every state, under each state's trust code or common law of trusts. The details — execution requirements, default rules, creditor provisions — vary by state, which is why the governing-law clause in the indenture matters and why local review is wise for large estates.

Does a private express trust need to be registered or filed anywhere?

No. Creating the trust requires no state filing and no court approval. Separate obligations still exist — an irrevocable trust typically needs its own tax ID number and tax returns, and real estate transferred into the trust is recorded by deed — but the trust instrument itself stays private.

Can the grantor also be the trustee and a beneficiary?

The grantor can serve as trustee, and often does in a revocable family trust. One hard rule: the sole trustee cannot be the sole beneficiary of the same trust — legal and equitable title would merge and the trust collapses. At least one other party must hold a real interest.

What is the difference between a private express trust and a living trust?

A living (inter vivos) trust is simply an express trust created during the grantor's lifetime rather than by a will. Most private express trusts discussed here are living trusts. "Living" describes when it is made; "express" describes how — by deliberate written intent.

Will a private express trust protect my assets from a lawsuit?

Not in the way it is often advertised. A revocable trust provides essentially no creditor protection for the grantor, and even irrevocable structures are fact-specific, state-specific, and voidable if funded to defeat an existing claim. What the trust reliably delivers is probate avoidance, continuity of management, and privacy — plan on those, and get advice before expecting more.
