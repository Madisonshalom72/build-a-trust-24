Stipulations — Conditions You Can Build Into the Trust

Stipulations are conditions the Grantor writes into the trust itself — rules the Trustee must run by, and conditions a beneficiary’s interest carries. Every stipulation below is selectable in this builder’s standard-provisions library (Step 2), and the ones you pick print into the Trust Indenture Agreement as formal clauses.

Stipulations for Trustees

  • Earn the Trustee Badge first. No one serves as Trustee until they complete the Trustee Training track and earn the Trustee Badge — a grantor-set education condition, written into the trust.
  • Annual accounting. The Trustee delivers a full written accounting to every beneficiary each year, within 60 days of year-end.
  • No self-dealing. The Trustee may not buy from, sell to, lend to, or otherwise transact with the Trust for personal benefit.
  • Trustee bond (or waiver). The Trustee serves with a fiduciary bond protecting the beneficiaries — unless the bond is waived in writing.
  • Compensation cap / fee schedule. Trustee fees are capped to a schedule — no open-ended “reasonable fee” drift.
  • Dual signatures. Distributions above the set amount need two signatures — Trustee plus a co-signer.
  • Successor step-in on incapacity. If the Trustee cannot serve, the named successor steps in immediately and the records change hands fast.
  • Removal for cause. Beneficiaries or the Protector can remove a Trustee for cause — dishonesty, incapacity, or failure to account.

Stipulations for Beneficiaries

  • Spendthrift protection. A beneficiary’s interest cannot be sold, assigned, or pledged — by the beneficiary, or reached ahead of distribution.
  • Milestone distributions. Principal unlocks when a beneficiary hits milestones the Grantor sets — education, first home, business start.
  • HEMS-only distributions. Distributions for health, education, maintenance, and support — and for nothing else.
  • Withholding during substance abuse or legal trouble. The Trustee may pause a beneficiary’s distributions during addiction or serious legal trouble — and may pay for treatment directly.
  • No-contest stipulation. A beneficiary who contests the Trust and loses forfeits their share.
  • Earn the Beneficiary Badge first. Discretionary distributions begin after the beneficiary completes Beneficiary Training and earns the Beneficiary Badge.
  • Age-staged distributions. A beneficiary’s share releases in stages at set ages instead of all at once.

The Contracts Engine — Grow the Trust Through Contracts

        
The fastest way to grow to a million dollars is through CONTRACTS.

        
Savings alone crawl. Contracts move money in large, scheduled amounts. That is how insurance works — a contract signed today pays a benefit tomorrow — and a trust can be built the same way: the trust owns contracts, is paid by contracts, and stands as beneficiary of contracts. Four contract lanes feed a trust:

      
        

          
1 · Life Insurance Contracts
The ILIT pattern: a trust owns life insurance policies, or stands as their beneficiary, so a policy’s benefit funds the trust when it pays — contract money landing in the trust, not in probate hands. One policy can fund a trust at a level decades of saving would struggle to reach.

          
2 · Contractual Income Streams
Rents, royalties, service agreements, and payment contracts can be assigned into the trust, so the income the contract produces is paid to the trustee for the beneficiaries — month after month, by agreement, not by effort.

          
3 · Promissory Notes & Private Agreements
Loans the trust makes are documented with promissory notes held as trust assets. The note is the asset; the payments are the growth. Private agreements, properly written and signed, become part of the trust’s corpus.

          
4 · Business Contracts
Business revenue run through the trust — the trust holds the business interest and the contracts it signs — turns ordinary commerce into trust funding, under the trustee’s management.

        

        
Put the lanes together and you get the site’s headline model — the Instant Millionaire Trust Fund Builder: families contribute to Trust #1, and Trust #2 stands as beneficiary of the insurance policies, so the contract money flows into a trust built to hold it. How to Put a House in a Land Trust — Privacy, Probate & Clean Transfers

        
Learning how to put a house in a land trust starts with one idea: the trust holds the deed, you hold the benefit. A land trust for rental property keeps your name off the public title while the property itself stays exactly where it is — and when you put rental property in a trust this way, the beneficial interest becomes personal property you can assign on paper, with no new deed every time it changes hands. Below: the structure, the key parts, how a land trust deed transfer works, and the generator that splits the units.

      
        

          
What a Land Trust Is
A land trust is a title-holding trust: the trustee holds legal title to a piece of real estate, and the beneficiaries hold the beneficial interest — which the law treats as personal property, not real property. The property is managed, rented, and sold by direction of the beneficiaries, but the deed at the courthouse names the trustee, not you. That separation — title in the trust, benefit in your hands — is the whole machine.

          
What It’s Used For
Holding real estate privately — your name stays off the public record. Avoiding probate on the property: the beneficial interest passes under the trust agreement instead of through the court. Cleaner transfers: assign the beneficial interest on paper instead of recording a new deed. Estate planning for rentals and single properties — and the investor deal structures taught in the Real Estate & Land Trusts training track: subject-to, seller financing, and lease options.

          
Land Trust vs. Living Trust
A revocable living trust is a full estate plan: it holds many kinds of assets and directs your whole estate. A land trust is narrower and more mechanical — usually one property per trust, focused on title and privacy, with the power of direction concentrated in the beneficiaries. A land trust is not an asset-protection force field: it gives privacy and clean title mechanics. Liability protection is a different job — that is the LLC and insurance conversation, taught straight in the training track.

          
The Key Parts
1 · The trust agreement — the private rulebook naming the trustee, the beneficiaries, and the units. 2 · The deed into the trust — the recorded document that moves title to the trustee. 3 · The trustee — holds title and acts only on written direction. 4 · The beneficiaries — hold the units of beneficial interest. 5 · The power of direction — the power of direction land trust beneficiaries hold to direct the trustee in writing: sign this, sell that, distribute here.

Trust Funds & Businesses — Two Ways a Trust and a Business Connect

        
A trust and a business can be wired together in two opposite directions, and they are not the same arrangement. In the first, the trust owns the business — ownership sits inside the trust, and business profits flow up into the trust. In the second, the business is a beneficiary of the trust — the trust names the business to receive distributions, and money flows down into the business. Many families run both at once through a holding-company structure. Here is how each one is built, how the money moves, and how you operate it day to day.


        

          

            
Structure A · The Trust Owns the Business

            
Who owns what: The trustee, in the trustee’s capacity for the trust, holds the business interest — the membership units of an LLC, or the shares of a corporation. The trust is the owner / member / shareholder on paper; the business keeps its own name, EIN, bank accounts, licenses, and contracts.

            
How it gets there: The owner assigns the membership interest or shares to the trustee in writing, the LLC’s operating agreement (or the corporation’s stock ledger) is updated to name the trustee of the trust as the holder, and the assignment is filed with the trust’s records in Schedule A. An interest that was never actually assigned was never funded into the trust.

            
What it does: Business profits are distributed to the owner — the trust — so business earnings land inside the trust structure, to be held, reinvested, or distributed to the trust’s beneficiaries under the indenture. Ownership can also pass under the trust’s terms at the owner’s death, outside probate, instead of through a will.

          

          

            
Structure B · The Business Is a Beneficiary of the Trust

            
Who receives what: The trust’s indenture names a business — an LLC or corporation, identified by its legal name — as a beneficiary. The trustee may then make distributions to the business, on the terms the indenture states: the purpose, the standard, and any limits are written in advance.

            
How it gets there: The business is named in the beneficiary provisions when the trust is drafted (or added where the trust allows it), it can be allocated units of beneficial interest like any other beneficiary in the builder above, and it is recorded in the Trust Fund CRM beneficiary register and the units ledger, with its own Certificate of Beneficial Interest.

            
What it does: Trust money funds the business on purpose — equipment, inventory, a vehicle, working capital, a down payment — without the money passing through an individual first. The distribution is a trust distribution to a named beneficiary, documented by the trustee, and received into the business’s own account.

          

        

        
The difference in one line: in Structure A the business belongs to the trust and profits flow up; in Structure B the trust supports the business and distributions flow down. The paperwork, the tax picture, and who controls what are different for each — which is exactly why the structure has to be chosen deliberately, not assumed.


        
The Holding-Company Setup — Trust Fund + Holding LLC, Together

        
The model this site teaches for running Structure A at scale puts an LLC between the trust and the day-to-day businesses: a holding company. The trust owns the holding company; the holding company owns the operating businesses. Each layer has one job:

        

          
Layer 1 · The Trust FundThe owner on top. The trustee holds the holding company for the beneficiaries. This is the estate and governance layer — the indenture, the units, the succession plan.

          
↓ owns 100% of ↓

          
Layer 2 · The Holding Company LLCHolds, it does not operate. It owns the membership interests in the businesses below, holds contracts and valuable assets (equipment, intellectual property, notes), and receives the profits that flow up. It signs no customers and carries no day-to-day operating risk of its own.

          
↓ owns ↓

          
Layer 3 · The Operating BusinessesThe businesses that do the work — they serve customers, sign service contracts, hire, and invoice. Each operating business is its own LLC or company, so one business’s problems stay inside that business.

        

        
Why the middle layer exists: it keeps ownership in one place (the trust only has to own one thing — the holding company), it keeps risk separated (operating liabilities stay in the operating companies, away from the trust’s other assets), and it makes the whole structure transferable (add a business by having the holding company acquire it; pass the structure on under the trust’s terms). The same holding LLC can also be the business named as a beneficiary in Structure B — the trust can own the holding company and make distributions to it for a stated purpose.

The Waterfall Method — How the Money Flows, Tier by Tier

        
A waterfall is the order money is allowed to move. Nothing skips a tier: each level is filled, in writing, before money drops to the next. This is the waterfall for the holding-company setup (Structure A), with the reverse flow for Structure B noted at the end.

        

          
1
Tier 1 · Money Comes In at the Operating LevelCustomers pay the operating businesses — sales, service contracts, rents, and royalties land in each operating company’s own bank account. No trust money and no personal money is mixed in at this tier.

          
2
Tier 2 · The Operating Business Pays Its Own Way FirstOut of its revenue, each operating business pays its operating expenses, payroll, insurance, and taxes, and keeps a working reserve. Only what is left is profit available to move.

          
3
Tier 3 · Profit Flows Up to the Holding CompanyThe operating company distributes its net profit to its owner — the holding company LLC — as an owner distribution, recorded in both companies’ books. This is the step the Profit & Loss Calculator above measures.

          
4
Tier 4 · The Holding Company Distributes to the TrustThe holding company, after its own expenses and reserves, distributes to its owner — the trust. The trustee receives it as trust income and logs it in the trust’s accounting and the CRM / Vault records.

          
5
Tier 5 · The Trustee Distributes Under the IndentureLast tier, on purpose. The trustee applies the trust’s own order: keep the trust’s reserve, reinvest (including back into the businesses, Tier 1, when the indenture allows), then distribute to the beneficiaries according to their units and the distribution standard you selected — HEMS, staged, or discretionary. Every distribution is logged with its reason and the provision it was made under.

        

        
The reverse waterfall (Structure B): when the business is the beneficiary, money starts at Tier 5 and flows down — the trustee makes a documented distribution from the trust to the named business for the purpose stated in the indenture, the business receives it into its own account, and it is spent and accounted for at the operating level. Same discipline, opposite direction: written purpose first, money second.

How You Operate Each One, Day to Day

        

          

            
Operating Structure A · Trust Owns the Business

            
    
              
  • Sign as trustee. When the trust acts as owner, the trustee signs in that capacity — “Trustee of the [Trust Name]” — never as an individual owner.
    • 
              
  • Separate money, always. The business keeps its own accounts, books, and EIN. Trust funds are never used as the business’s checking account, and business revenue is never deposited personally.
    • 
              
  • Paper the ownership. Keep the signed assignment of the membership interest / shares, the updated operating agreement or stock ledger, and the trust’s Schedule A together in the Vault.
    • 
              
  • Document every step of the waterfall. Each distribution up the tiers is recorded in the books of the company paying it and the books of the layer receiving it, on the date it happens.
    • 
              
  • Run the business normally underneath. Customers, contracts, licenses, and insurance stay in the operating business’s name — customers should never notice the trust on top.
    • 
              
  • Review annually. At the trustee’s annual review: confirm the ownership records still match, premiums and filings are current, and the waterfall order in the operating agreement still matches how money actually moves.
    • 
            

          

          

            
Operating Structure B · Business as Beneficiary

            
    
              
  • Check the indenture before every distribution. The trustee confirms the business is a named beneficiary, the purpose is one the trust allows, and any standard or limit is met — then distributes.
    • 
              
  • Write the purpose down. Each distribution is logged with its amount, date, and stated business purpose (equipment, inventory, working capital), and filed with the trust’s distribution records.
    • 
              
  • Receive it as the business. The money lands in the business’s own account and is booked as a trust distribution received — not as customer revenue, and not as a personal deposit to the owner.
    • 
              
  • Spend it on the stated purpose. The business keeps receipts and records showing the distribution was used for the purpose the trustee documented.
    • 
              
  • Keep the register current. The business’s beneficiary entry, units, and distribution history stay up to date in the Trust Fund CRM and the units ledger, alongside the individual beneficiaries.
    • 
              
  • Re-confirm the arrangement yearly. If the business is sold, dissolved, or renamed, the beneficiary designation has to be addressed — a distribution cannot go to a business that no longer exists under that name.
    •

How to Start a Non-Profit — and Its Tax Benefits

        
A non-profit is an organization with a mission instead of owners — and it is built in two halves: your state creates the organization, and the IRS decides whether it is tax-exempt. Forming a nonprofit corporation does not by itself make anything tax-exempt; the benefits below flow from the federal 501(c)(3) determination, not the state filing. Here is how to start a non-profit, in the order the steps unlock.


        
The 7 Formation Steps, in Brief

        
    
          
  • 1. Choose the purpose and the name. Write the mission in a sentence or two, then check the name against your state’s business records.
    • 
          
  • 2. Incorporate with the state. File nonprofit articles of incorporation — with exempt-purpose and asset-dedication language in from the start, the way the IRS expects for a future 501(c)(3).
    • 
          
  • 3. Get an EIN. The organization’s own tax ID (Form SS-4; the online application is free) — needed even with no employees.
    • 
          
  • 4. Set up the board and bylaws. Name the initial directors (many states require at least three), adopt bylaws, hold the first meeting, keep minutes.
    • 
          
  • 5. Apply to the IRS for tax-exempt status. Form 1023, or the shorter 1023-EZ for small organizations. Only the IRS determination letter makes it a recognized 501(c)(3).
    • 
          
  • 6. Finish the state layer. State income-tax exemption, sales/property exemptions where offered, and charitable-solicitation registration before fundraising, where your state requires it.
    • 
          
  • 7. Open the books. A bank account in the organization’s name and EIN, bookkeeping that separates restricted funds, and the annual filings calendared. Formation is a day; administration is every year after.
    • 
        


        
The Tax Benefits, Plainly

        

          
Federal Income-Tax Exemption
Under § 501(a), a recognized 501(c)(3) pays no federal income tax on income related to its exempt purpose.

          
Deductible Donations
Contributions to a 501(c)(3) are deductible for donors under § 170 (for donors who itemize) — which makes fundraising materially easier.

          
Grant Eligibility
Most foundation and government grant programs will only fund recognized 501(c)(3) organizations.

          
State Tax Exemptions
Many states exempt 501(c)(3)s from state income or franchise tax, and some extend sales- or property-tax exemptions — these vary widely by state and usually need their own applications.

        

        
The limit that defines the whole structure: no private inurement — no part of a non-profit’s earnings may go to a founder, director, or any other private individual, and if it ever closes, its assets must pass to another exempt purpose. Income from a regular business not substantially related to the mission is taxed anyway (UBIT, §§ 511–514), and the exemption is maintained annually — most 501(c)(3)s file a Form 990 every year, and three straight years of not filing revokes it automatically. Run beside a trust, the pairing is the one this site teaches above: the trust holds the private side, the non-profit carries the mission — and a trust can name a 501(c)(3) as a beneficiary.

The Million Dollar Trust Roadmap

        
A staged path from a first deposit to a seven-figure trust. The dollar figures are illustrative scenario arithmetic — examples of how the stages stack, not a promise or guarantee of any result.

        

          
Stage 1 · FormChoose the structure, draft the Trust Indenture Agreement, name trustees and beneficiaries. Cost of entry: the paperwork done right.

          
Stage 2 · FundTransfer the first assets in — a home, accounts, business interests. An unfunded trust controls nothing; funding is what makes it real. Illustrative start: $10,000–$50,000 of assets titled to the trust.

          
Stage 3 · SystematizeRecurring contributions on a schedule. Illustrative math: $500/month at a hypothetical 6% grows to roughly $98,000 in 10 years — arithmetic, not a forecast.

          
Stage 4 · The Contracts EngineThe growth step. Put contracts to work: insurance policies owned by or payable to the trust, income streams assigned in, notes and business contracts held as assets. Illustrative: ten $100,000 policies payable to Trust #2 represent $1,000,000 of contract value standing behind the trust.

          
Stage 5 · Units & GovernanceDivide the trust into units of beneficial interest, issue bond-paper certificates, keep the ledger, train the trustees, beneficiaries, and guardians, and review annually.

          
Stage 6 · Seven FiguresContributions + growth + contract proceeds, compounding inside one governed structure. The Instant Millionaire Trust Fund Builder calculator models exactly this stack.

Trust Creation Checklist

          
    
            
  • Choose the trust type that fits the goal (use the comparison and the AI Trust Specialist).
    • 
            
  • Name the grantor(s), initial trustee, and successor trustee.

    •

Funding Checklist

          
    
            
  • Record real estate by a new deed naming the trustee of the trust.
    • 
            
  • Retitle bank and brokerage accounts, or list the trust as payable-on-death beneficiary where appropriate.
    • 
            
  • Assign business interests and LLC units to the trustee in writing.
    • 
            
  • Execute a general assignment for personal property.
    • 
            
  • Name the trust (or an ILIT) as beneficiary on insurance and retirement accounts only with professional advice — retirement accounts have special tax rules.
    • 
            
  • Keep every transfer document with the indenture; an unfunded trust controls nothing.
    •

    •

    • 
            
  • Review investments against the prudent investor standard.
    • 
            
  • Log every distribution with its reason and the provision it was made under.
    • 
            
  • Confirm insurance premiums are paid and beneficiary designations still name the right trust.
    • 
            
  • Update the units ledger for any transfer, birth, death, or marriage.
