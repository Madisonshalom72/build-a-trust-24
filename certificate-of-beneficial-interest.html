The Certificate of Beneficial Interest

A certificate of beneficial interest is the document that evidences a beneficiary's ownership units in a trust. What it certifies, how units work, how they transfer, and why the trust's ownership ledger never leaves its private records.

What a certificate of beneficial interest certifies

A certificate of beneficial interest is the formal document a trustee issues to evidence a beneficiary's ownership share in a trust. A trust splits its ownership two ways: the trustee holds legal title to the property, and the beneficiaries hold the equitable — or beneficial — interest. The certificate is the paper proof of that second half. It certifies that the named holder owns a stated number of units of beneficial interest in the named trust, issued under the trust's indenture agreement, on a stated date, over the trustee's signature and the trust's seal.

Think of it the way a stock certificate once worked for a corporation: the certificate does not create the ownership — the trust's records do — but it evidences it in a form a holder can keep, present, and transfer. In a private express trust, where nothing about the ownership is filed anywhere public, that evidentiary role matters even more.

Units of beneficial interest, explained

Rather than describe each beneficiary's share as a fraction of every asset, many trusts divide the beneficial interest into units — conventionally 100 units representing the whole. A beneficiary holding 40 units holds a 40% beneficial interest; distributions of income and principal are calculated by units, and partial transfers are simple arithmetic instead of deed-drafting. When new property is contributed or an interest is divided among children, the unit ledger absorbs the change cleanly. The certificate states the units; the trust's private ledger — the register of certificates — states who holds every unit, and the two are kept in agreement by the trustee.

What appears on the certificate

  • The trust's name and date of creation, tying the certificate to one specific indenture.
  • A certificate number, matching the entry in the trust's register, so any certificate can be traced and any forgery detected.
  • The holder's name and the number of units certified.
  • A statement of the interest's character — that the holder's rights are governed by the indenture, and any transfer restrictions the indenture imposes.
  • The trustee's signature, the date of issue, and the trust seal — the marks that make the document an official act of the trust rather than a private note.

How transfers work

A beneficial interest is personal property, and it moves the way the indenture says it moves. Typically: the holder endorses the certificate or signs a separate assignment, the old certificate is surrendered to the trustee, the trustee cancels it in the register and issues a new certificate to the transferee. Until that register entry is made, the trustee continues to treat the recorded holder as the owner — for distributions, notices, and votes. Many family trusts restrict transfer: no transfers outside the family, a right of first refusal for the trust or the other holders, or trustee consent. Those restrictions, printed on the certificate and written in the indenture, are what keep a private trust private across generations.

Why the names stay in the trust's private records

Here is the design point most explanations miss. The certificate shows units and a holder; the complete ownership picture — every holder, every unit, every transfer — lives only in the trust's register, kept with the indenture in the trust's own vault. No state database lists a private trust's beneficiaries, no certificate is recorded at a courthouse, and third parties who need assurance receive a certification of trust that confirms the trustee's authority without exposing the ledger. Privacy here is not secrecy for its own sake; it is the ordinary confidentiality of a family's internal ownership records, the same confidentiality a private company's stock ledger enjoys.

What a certificate is not

A certificate of beneficial interest evidences ownership inside one private trust. It is not a publicly traded security, not an investment product offered to the public, and not a promise of profit — its value is simply the value of the trust property behind the units, no more and no less. Offering beneficial interests to outside investors raises securities-law questions that a family trust structure is not built to answer. It also changes nothing about the trust's obligations: a certificate does not shield trust property from the trust's own lawful debts, and it does not alter anyone's tax position.

Frequently asked questions

Is a certificate of beneficial interest the same as a stock certificate?

Similar in function, different in law. Both evidence an ownership interest recorded in an issuer's register. But a stock certificate represents shares in a corporation — a state-created entity — while a certificate of beneficial interest represents units in a trust, a private contractual arrangement. The rights behind each come from entirely different documents: a charter and bylaws on one side, a trust indenture on the other.

What happens if a certificate is lost or destroyed?

The register, not the paper, is the controlling record. The trustee can require an affidavit of loss, cancel the lost certificate number in the register, and issue a replacement marked as such. This is one reason the numbered register matters: a lost certificate cannot be quietly duplicated, because its number is already spoken for.

Can units of beneficial interest be divided or gifted?

Yes, subject to the indenture's transfer rules. A holder can typically gift units — to children, for example — by assignment, with the trustee recording the new holder and issuing a new certificate. Gift and estate tax consequences can attach to the transfer itself, so the tax side deserves advice before the paperwork, not after.

Do beneficiaries have to hold certificates at all?

No. A trust can run entirely on its register, with interests stated in the indenture and no certificates issued. Certificates add formality and clarity — each holder has a document to keep with their own records — which is why unitized trusts issue them, but the beneficial interest exists because the indenture creates it, certificate or not.

Can a beneficiary sell their units to an outsider?

Only if the indenture permits it, and most private family trusts restrict exactly that — through consent requirements, rights of first refusal, or outright limits to family transferees. Check the transfer section of the indenture and the legend on the certificate before assuming an interest can be sold.
