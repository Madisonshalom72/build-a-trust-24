The Trust Indenture Agreement, Section by Section

A trust indenture agreement is the written instrument that creates the trust — the parties, the property, the trustee's powers, and the terms of distribution, all in one founding document. Here is what a complete one contains.

What a trust indenture agreement is

A trust indenture agreement is the founding written instrument of a trust: the document in which the grantor declares the trust into existence, transfers property to the trustee, and writes down the rules the trustee must follow. Lawyers also call this document the trust instrument — the two terms describe the same thing, the single piece of paper (or executed set) that a bank, a court, or a successor trustee will read to learn what the trust is and what it may do.

One clarification, because the search results will not give it to you: in the finance pages that dominate this phrase, "indenture" usually means a bond indenture — the contract between a bond issuer and a bond trustee under the Trust Indenture Act of 1939. That is a different instrument for a different job. In trust creation, the older and original sense applies: an indenture is a formal deed executed in counterparts, and a trust indenture agreement is the deed that creates a private express trust. This page is about that document.

What goes inside: the sections, one by one

  • Name and declaration. The trust's name, the date, and the grantor's declaration of intent — the words that make the trust express rather than implied. This section also states whether the trust is revocable or irrevocable.
  • The parties. Full legal names of the grantor, the trustee, and the beneficiaries, with the role each one holds. Ambiguity here is the most expensive kind: every later dispute starts with who, exactly, was meant.
  • The trust property. A description of the initial property transferred to the trustee, with a schedule of assets attached as an exhibit. Property not listed and never transferred is property the trust does not hold — the schedule is where funding begins.
  • Trustee powers and duties. What the trustee may do — hold, invest, lease, sell, open accounts, hire professionals — and the fiduciary duties that bind them: loyalty to the beneficiaries, prudent management, and a duty to account. Powers not granted may not exist when the trustee needs them.
  • Distributions. Who receives income and principal, when, and under what standards — outright, at stated ages or milestones, or in the trustee's discretion for health, education, maintenance, and support. This is the section beneficiaries actually live under.
  • Successor trustees. The line of succession if the trustee resigns, is removed, becomes incapacitated, or dies — plus how a successor accepts the office. A trust with no successor named is a trust one accident away from a court appointment.
  • Beneficial interests. How ownership is divided into units of beneficial interest and evidenced by certificates of beneficial interest, and the rules for transferring those units.
  • Amendment, revocation, and termination. Who may change the instrument, how, and what ends the trust — a date, an event, or the distribution of all its property.
  • Governing law and execution. The state law that controls interpretation, followed by signatures — grantor and trustee — typically notarized, which is what makes the instrument presentable to banks and title companies.

Why this one document carries so much weight

A trust has no charter, no state-issued certificate, and no public file. The indenture is the trust, in the only form the outside world can examine. When a trustee opens a bank account, sells trust real estate, or defends a distribution, the trust instrument is the evidence. Gaps in it — unnamed successors, silent distribution terms, unsigned pages — do not stay theoretical; they surface at the worst moments, in front of institutions with no patience for them. A complete indenture is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the operating system the trustee runs for decades.

Read this before you sign one

A generated indenture is a strong starting draft, not a substitute for judgment. State law controls execution formalities and default rules, and tax treatment turns on choices — revocable versus irrevocable, grantor versus non-grantor — that deserve a deliberate decision, not a default. A trust also does not erase taxes, judgments, or child support, and funding one to defeat an existing creditor can be unwound. Have a qualified attorney in your state review the final instrument before you fund the trust, especially where real estate, business interests, or special-needs beneficiaries are involved.

Frequently asked questions

Is a trust indenture agreement the same as a trust instrument?

Yes. "Trust instrument" is the general term for the document that creates and governs a trust; "trust indenture agreement" is the formal name used when the instrument is executed as an agreement between the grantor and the trustee. Build A Trust 24 uses the indenture name for the document its generator produces.

How is this different from a bond indenture?

A bond indenture is a debt contract between a bond issuer and a trustee for bondholders, governed for public offerings by the Trust Indenture Act of 1939. A trust indenture agreement creates a private trust that holds property for beneficiaries. Same legal ancestor — a formal deed — entirely different purpose.

Does the indenture need to be notarized or recorded?

Notarization is not universally required for validity, but it is standard practice and many institutions expect it. The indenture itself is not recorded publicly — that privacy is a feature of the structure. Deeds transferring real estate into the trust are recorded, and those deeds, not the indenture, are what appear in the public record.

Can a trust indenture agreement be changed after signing?

A revocable trust's indenture can be amended or revoked by the grantor following the method the document itself prescribes. An irrevocable trust's terms are far harder to change — typically requiring beneficiary consent, a court order, or a decanting into a new trust where state law allows it. Choose the type with that permanence in mind.

What happens if the indenture never gets funded?

The trust exists on paper but holds nothing, controls nothing, and avoids no probate. Funding — actually transferring the scheduled assets into the trustee's name — is a separate, essential step after signing. The schedule of assets in the indenture is the checklist for that transfer.
